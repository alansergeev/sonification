Two recursive systems · made audible

Structures that repeat,
sounds that never settle.

These studies turn two mathematical ideas into instruments. First, the Cantor set removes the middle third of every surviving interval. Then a Shepard spectrum folds a descending family of partials back into itself. Each diagram is driven by the same values as its sound.

01 / The Cantor set, one iteration at a time

Hear the set disappear.

Begin with a whole interval. Divide it into thirds, retain the outer two, and remove the middle. Each recording adds one more depth to the same process. Cyan shows the thirds that continue; magenta shows the thirds removed at that depth. Both are sonified by one shared voice system—neither receives special treatment.

ITERATION 01The whole interval

One band and one pitch establish the scale of the demonstration.

The first cut creates three equal events. The outer thirds survive, but all three are heard with identical synthesis settings.

ITERATION 02The first removal

The middle third becomes visible as absence while the two edges continue.

Two intervals become six events, and the gaps begin to establish a rhythm.

ITERATION 03Recursion becomes rhythm

Each surviving edge now performs the same three-way decision.

As the intervals shrink, pitch rises. The rule does not change; only its scale does.

ITERATION 04A lattice of thirds

The pattern is still countable, but it is beginning to read as texture.

More silence enters the picture, even as the number of sounding decisions grows.

ITERATION 05Approaching the dust

Fine repetitions collect at the edge of visual and audible resolution.

Every solid bar is split into equal thirds. The outer thirds continue into the next row; the middle third ends there. That geometric difference is visible in colour, but it does not create a second synthesiser. A single gain, waveform, and scheduling rule handles every segment.

02 / A spectrum with no apparent floor

The line descends.
The spectrum returns.

A Shepard glide stacks related partials across a frequency-pinned window. As components approach the edge they fade, while counterparts enter at the other side. The ear follows motion through a spectrum that continuously rebuilds itself.
